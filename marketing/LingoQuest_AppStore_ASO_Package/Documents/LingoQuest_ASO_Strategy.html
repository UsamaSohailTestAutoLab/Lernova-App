    01Executive summary

    LingoQuest is a complete, working language-learning app with a genuine differentiator that its current positioning does not claim loudly enough. Ten courses, a structured Path, and ten distinct mini-games built from the same vocabulary are all live in the build I ran. The store listing should be built on the games.

    The five things that matter

    1. The USP is Learn → Practice → Play, and the app says so itself. Its own onboarding carries a slide titled "Learn → Practice → Play". That is the positioning; it just needs to be on the store page. Fact
    2. Ten games is the rarest asset here. Of the six major competitors I checked, only Drops puts "Games" in its App Store title. The games cluster is the one place LingoQuest can win head terms rather than fight for them. Fact
    3. The app is English-only, and that caps localisation. There is no lib/l10n, no supportedLocales, and every course teaches its language from English. Translating store metadata into nine languages would buy installs from people who cannot use the product. Risk
    4. The icon is the weakest asset in the package. A green parrot on a green field, full-body, with a pointer that vanishes below 60px. It scores 3/10 on small-size readability. Estimate
    5. Content depth is the real launch constraint. Nine lessons per language across three units. That is enough to prove the loop, not enough to hold a 30-day streak — and the achievements screen advertises a 30-day streak badge. Fact
    Recommended positioning

    Brand: LingoQuest  ·  USP: Learn languages by playing games  ·  Proof: 10 games, 10 languages, one vocabulary.

    The pattern, and the gap

    Five of six spend their subtitle listing languages. It is the default move, and it means every one of them competes on the same terms with a bigger budget. Only Drops claims games in its title — and Drops is a vocabulary-only app with no lesson path.

    LingoQuest can claim something none of them can: a structured path and a games arcade that share one vocabulary. Drops has games without the path; Duolingo and Babbel have the path with gamified lessons rather than actual games.

    Competitor weaknesses worth targeting Estimate

    • Duolingo's scope drift. Its subtitle now sells maths, music and chess. Learners who want a language app and nothing else are an addressable segment.
    • Price. Babbel's listed IAP runs to $107.99/yr and Memrise's to $329.99 lifetime. LingoQuest's proposed $30/yr is materially below the field.
    • Drops caps at vocabulary. No sentences, no path, no grammar. LingoQuest has sentence building and a unit structure.

    Production notes

    • Two native sets, not one resized set. The App Store set was captured with the device display overridden to 1320 × 2868 @ 3×, so Flutter laid the UI out at 440 × 956 pt — iPhone 16 Pro Max geometry exactly. The Play set was captured at the phone's own 1080 × 2400. Neither is the other one stretched, and the two genuinely differ: the iPhone frames fit more on screen, and Home shows a "continue the Path" row the Android frames do not.
    • Device chrome: Android's status bar and gesture pill were painted out with the app's own background colour, and the iOS status bar, Dynamic Island and home indicator are drawn in the template — presentation, the same convention every App Store listing uses. Everything inside them is the running app.
    • Size: 1320 × 2868 px, the 6.9-inch iPhone class. App Store Connect scales that down for every smaller iPhone, so one set covers the family. (spec)
    • Type: headlines set in Baloo 2 and Source-side copy in Inter — the app's own two faces, so the store page and the product look like the same product.
    • Device frame: a plain CSS-drawn rounded rectangle. No manufacturer's handset artwork is used.
    • Palette rhythm: light green → deep green → light green → amber, so the set reads as a designed sequence in the carousel rather than ten variations of one card.

    10App icon review

    The current icon is the full-body parrot mascot, centred on a solid mid-green field, holding a pointer stick.

    Scored out of 10 Estimate
    CriterionScoreReasoning
    Brand recognition5Recognisable once known, but a cartoon bird is not a distinctive mark in this category
    Language association2Nothing in the icon signals language. A parrot implies repeating, which is a stretch for a cold viewer
    Game association2The pointer stick reads as teaching, not play. Nothing signals a game
    Small-size readability3Fails below ~60px: green-on-green kills the silhouette and the pointer disappears entirely
    Uniqueness4Green cartoon bird mascots are crowded here, and proximity to the category leader's owl is a liability rather than an asset
    App Store attractiveness5Clean and professional, but does not stop a scroll
    Total21/60

    Recommended redesign Recommendation

    1. Crop to head and shoulders. Fill 80–85% of the canvas with the face. At 29px, a face still reads; a full body does not.
    2. Change the ground to amber. The brand already owns #FFB020, and a green parrot on amber has the contrast the current icon lacks. It also separates the icon from every green competitor tile in a search result.
    3. Drop the pointer stick. It is the first thing lost at small sizes and it sells "teacher", which is the opposite of the positioning.
    4. Add one game cue, subtly. The glasses lenses can carry a faint bubble highlight that echoes Word Bubble without becoming a second object.
    Constraint

    No owl, no green-bird-on-green, no speech-bubble-with-a-globe. The first invites a comparison LingoQuest loses; the last two are the category's two most generic icons.

    Why this hook

    I tested three openers. "Stop studying. Start playing." is punchier but picks a fight with studying, which alienates the serious-learner segment. "Learn. Play. Progress." is tidy but says nothing concrete. "Learn a language by playing" wins because it is the whole product in five words and it is literally true of the frame behind it.

    Where to spend first Recommendation

    Spanish, French and Japanese.

    • Spanish — the largest learn-a-language demand in the English-speaking world, and LingoQuest's most complete course (it is the reference course every other one was generated against).
    • French — second-largest English-speaker demand, and the French course is the only other one with content beyond the base generation pass.
    • Japanese — smaller volume, but the audience skews young, game-literate and app-native, which is precisely the segment a games-first pitch converts best. It is also where "learn through games" faces the least entrenched competition.

    Arabic and Chinese are deliberately not in the first three. Both need script handling and right-to-left support that I did not verify, and both carry a higher risk of a one-star review for a rendering fault than they carry upside.

    18Localisation strategy

    The dependency that governs this section

    Apple and Google both let you localise a listing without localising the app. Doing that here would put a Spanish-language store page in front of a Spanish speaker who then opens an English-only interface teaching Spanish from English. That is a refund, a one-star review, and a permanent dent in the listing's conversion rate.

    Three tiers Recommendation

    TierLocalesAction nowUnblocks when
    A — ship nowen-US, en-GB, en-AU, en-CAFull metadata, all 10 screenshots, preview videoLive at launch

    The one thing I would fix before any of it

    Nine lessons per language is three units. A learner who takes the app at its word and builds a streak will exhaust Spanish inside a fortnight, and the achievements screen is advertising a 30-day streak badge they cannot earn on content alone. Every pound spent on ASO before that gap closes is buying installs into a leaky bucket.

    Ship the store package as specified, hold the paid acquisition until there is enough content to survive the streak the app is selling.
